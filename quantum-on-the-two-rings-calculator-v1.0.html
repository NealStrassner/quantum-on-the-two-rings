Quantum Computing on the Two Rings — every gate a setting, every probability a fold, every classical step a turn

Set a gate angle. The rings read the gate: half the centre distance is cos(θ/2), half the crossing distance is sin(θ/2). Beside every ring reading stands the textbook value, with the difference. Below, the forty tools of quantum computing computed one by one as the three moves.

Invented and discovered by Neal Strassner.

The setting. A gate angle θ is one placement of the rings with the crossings θ apart. The centres then stand 2cos(θ/2) apart and the crossings 2sin(θ/2) apart, and those two half-distances are the entries of every rotation gate: Rz has them as real and imaginary parts on its diagonal, Ry has them on and off the diagonal, Rx has cos and −i·sin, and the two-qubit Rzz and XX gates carry the same two numbers. The gate angle sits at ring position θ/12 (the half angle, six degrees a position); angles that are multiples of 12° sit on the top ring, every other angle on the ladder of finer rings, which carry the same sixty digits.

The certificate. The digits under the two crossings, at positions k and 60 − k, are equal for odd k and sum to ten for even k. At the cardinals (multiples of fifteen) both are 0 and the pair (F(n), F(n+1)) names which cardinal: (0,1), (0,7), (0,9), (0,3) at 0, 15, 30, 45.

The fold. Born's rule, P(0) = cos²(θ/2), is the half-angle law cos²(θ/2) = (1 + cos θ)/2: the amplitude is read at position k and the expectation value ⟨Z⟩ = cos θ at position 2k, the expectation ring, drawn on the right.

The turns. Merging two gates adds positions, with the landing digit fixed by the addition law. A gradient is the quarter turn: ±15 positions on the expectation ring, digit ×7 forward and ×3 back. The spinor's −1 after 360° is the half turn, digits summing to ten; 720° is the lap. The Pauli phases 1, i, −1, −i are the locked square n, n+15, n+30, n+45 with digits 1, 7, 9, 3 (from n = 1).

The instrument in four pictures the ground rules under the cards

Everything in the cards below uses four facts. Each is stated once here, with its picture, in the words of the standard formalism beside the ring's. The glossary of words and symbols is the drawer at the end.

1. The law of the settinggeometry

Two rings of radius one. The second slides along the line of centres until they cross with the crossings θ apart.
Why the lengths are the gate's entries: each crossing lies on both rings, so it is at distance 1 from both centres. The crossings sit at positions k = θ/12 and 60 − k, θ apart on the ring, so the ring setting for a gate of angle θ is the half angle θ/2: the crossing sits θ/2 above the line of centres, so the centres stand 2cos(θ/2) apart and the two crossings 2sin(θ/2) apart. Those are the entries of Rz(θ), Ry(θ) and Rx(θ). A position is 6° (sixty on the ring), the half angle θ/2 sits at position θ/12. The instrument and its law are the paper Two Sliding Rings: A Self-Checking Instrument that Reads Sine, Cosine and Tangent at One Setting, with its own calculator page.

2. Why Fibonacci, why sixtythe net

The marks on the rings are the last digits of the Fibonacci numbers: 0, 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 3 (13), 1 (21), 4 (34), 5, 9, 4, 3, 7, 0, 7, 7, 4, 1, 5, 6, 1, 7, 8, 5, 3, 8, 1, 9, 0, 9, 9, 8, 7, 5, 2, 7, 9, 6, 5, 1, 6, 7, 3, 0, 3, 3, 6, 9, 5, 4, 9, 3, 2, 5, 7, 2, 9, 1.
The two facts the ring rests on: the last digits repeat with period sixty (the Pisano period of 10), so sixty positions carry them all; and the digits pair off in sums of ten across the two cardinal axes, F(n) + F(60−n) ≡ 0 at even n and F(n) + F(30−n) ≡ 0 at odd n (mod 10). Draw each pair as a chord and every chord is level or upright: that grid of twenty-eight chords is Neal's Net, and its twin, joining the equal digits, completes the fourteen rectangles. The four cardinals 0, 15, 30, 45 carry the digit 0 and sit on no chord. The net, this grid and its reading as a checksum, is Strassner's discovery, stated and verified in Neal's Net, Version 3 (the paper), with its own calculator page.

3. The three digit lawsthe turns

Every turn of the ring carries its digit with it by a fixed rule, and every sum of positions has a predicted digit.
Written out, all mod 10: quarter turn F(n+15) ≡ 7·F(n); half turn F(n+30) ≡ 9·F(n) ≡ −F(n); three-quarter turn F(n+45) ≡ 3·F(n); and the addition law F(a+b) ≡ F(a)·F(b+1) + F(a−1)·F(b). Since 7² = 49 ≡ −1, the digit 7 is the ring's i, and the four digits d, 7d, 9d, 3d at n, n+15, n+30, n+45 are d times 1, i, −1, −i. That is the locked square, and it is what the Pauli frame rides.
In plain words: start at any position on the ring and read its digit. Move a quarter of the way round, clockwise, fifteen positions. The digit you land on is the digit you started with times 7, last digit kept. Move half way round, thirty positions, and the digit you land on is the starting digit times 9, last digit kept, which is the same as 10 minus the starting digit. Move three quarters of the way round and it is the starting digit times 3. In the picture the start is position 1 with the digit 1, so the four corners read 1, 7, 9, 3: the dim square joins them, and that square sits on the ring at every starting position, with its four digits always d, 7d, 9d, 3d. The same rule read twice is why 7 behaves as the ring's i: a quarter turn and another quarter turn is a half turn, and 7 × 7 = 49 ends in 9, which is −1 in last digits.

4. The certificate, and what it catchesthe guard

A quantum computer's classical half holds angles as positions: the virtual-Z phase accumulator of every qubit, the compiler's merged rotation, the Pauli frame. Beside each position the ring stores its digit pair (F(n), F(n+1)). Corrupt the position and the recomputed pair no longer matches the stored one.
corrupt the stored position 10 (the gate Rz(120°)):
position 10 holds Rz(120°) with its pair (5,9). Flip a bit, or write any other position, and read it back.
Coverage, counted here on every case: arbitrary corruption of a position, all 3540 cases: one digit catches 90.40%, the pair 100.00%, a mod-3 residue 67.80%. Single-bit upsets of the six-bit register, all 360: one digit 97.78%, the pair 100.00%, and a mod-3 residue also 100% (no power of two is a multiple of three). The pair is one to one with the sixty positions, which is why nothing escapes it; the register card below extends this to 32-bit phase words with three rings and to the ladder.

the symbols
θ (theta) the gate angle, in degrees: the angle a rotation gate turns a qubit through. Rz(θ), Ry(θ), Rx(θ) are the three rotation gates.
n a position on the ring: one of the sixty marks, numbered 0 to 59, six degrees apart, 0 at the left and counting clockwise.
F(n) the digit written at position n: the last digit of the n-th Fibonacci number. F(6) = 8 because the sixth Fibonacci number is 8; F(7) = 3 because the seventh is 13.
k the position of a gate: k = θ/12. A gate of angle θ turns the amplitudes by the half angle θ/2, and a position is 6 degrees, so θ/2 ÷ 6 = θ/12. Rz(72°) sits at k = 6; Rz(120°) at k = 10.
2k twice that position: the full angle θ, read at θ/6. Rz(72°) reads its expectation values at 2k = 12.
d any digit 0 to 9; 7d means seven times that digit, last digit kept.
(F(n), F(n+1)) the pair: the digit at a position and the digit at the next one. The sixty pairs are all different, so a pair identifies its position.
|0⟩, |1⟩ the two basis states of a qubit; cos(θ/2) and sin(θ/2) are their amplitudes after Ry(θ) on |0⟩.
P(0), P(1) the probabilities of reading 0 or 1: the squares of those amplitudes.
⟨Z⟩, ⟨X⟩ expectation values: the average of the ±1 outcomes of measuring Z or X many times. For the state above ⟨Z⟩ = cos θ and ⟨X⟩ = sin θ.
≡ (mod 10) equal in the last digit: 55 ≡ 5.
the words
the setting one placement of the two rings. The value is a length read off it: the centre line is 2cos(θ/2), the crossing line 2sin(θ/2).
the fold turning a product into a sum by the half-angle identity cos²(θ/2) = (1 + cos θ)/2. The amplitude is read at position k, the expectation value at position 2k.
the turn moving a position around the ring. Its digit follows by the laws of picture 3: a quarter turn multiplies the digit by 7, a half turn by 9, three quarters by 3.
amplitude ring the ring read at position k = θ/12. Its two half-lengths are the gate's entries and the qubit's amplitudes.
expectation ring the ring read at position 2k = θ/6, the full angle, where ⟨Z⟩ = cos θ and ⟨X⟩ = sin θ.
cardinal the positions 0, 15, 30 and 45, on the two axes. Each carries the digit 0 and no chord; the pair there names which cardinal it is: (0,1), (0,7), (0,9), (0,3). On a finer ring, any multiple of fifteen.
the ladder the finer rings for angles between the sixty marks. Level 1 has 3,600 positions of 0.1°, level 2 has 216,000, level L has 60·60^L, and every level carries the same sixty digits in the same order.
rung one position on a finer ring. The T gate, Rz(45°), is rung 225 of the 3,600 on level 1.
the vesica the setting with the centres one radius apart (the vesica piscis of geometry): crossings 120° apart, position 10, half-lengths ½ and √3/2. The quarter-turn vesica has its crossings 90° apart, position 7.5.
the net the twenty-eight chords joining each digit to the digit that makes ten with it: 60−n at an even position (an upright chord), 30−n at an odd one (a level chord). Neal's Net.
the twin the twenty-eight chords joining equal digits, the other mirror partner of each position. Net and twin together cut the ring into fourteen rectangles.
the locked square the four positions n, n+15, n+30, n+45 and their digits d, 7d, 9d, 3d, which are d times 1, i, −1, −i. The Pauli frame's phases ride it.
gnomon reading an angle read as a tangent on an upright line standing at position 30. The optimizer finds its best angle this way, where tan θ* = C/B.
the certificate the digit test on a setting: the two digits under the crossings are equal at an odd position and sum to ten at an even one, and the stored pair (F(n), F(n+1)) is recomputed on every read of a register.
